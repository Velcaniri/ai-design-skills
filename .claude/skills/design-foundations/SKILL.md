# Design Foundations

Treat design principles as lenses, not a required checklist. Start with audience, purpose, message, medium, and desired action. Establish what should be noticed first, next, and last.

- **Hierarchy and emphasis:** Give important information clear priority using scale, weight, position, color, contrast, and space. Avoid emphasizing everything.
- **Alignment and grid:** Align related elements to common edges, baselines, or a deliberate axis. Break alignment only when it communicates something useful.
- **Proximity and grouping:** Use spacing, shared regions, and consistent styling to show which items belong together.
- **Contrast and legibility:** Separate visual roles with differences in size, weight, color, shape, or texture. Check actual backgrounds and states.
- **Balance and proportion:** Distribute visual weight deliberately. Symmetry can feel stable; asymmetry can feel dynamic. No fixed ratio works for every design.
- **Repetition, rhythm, and unity:** Reuse type, color, spacing, and shape choices to create cohesion; vary them when it helps attention or flow.
- **Color:** Choose a purposeful palette that fits the brand and context. Associations vary across people and cultures; do not encode meaning with color alone.
- **Negative space:** Use empty space to separate groups, improve readability, and give focal elements room.
- **Movement and pattern:** Guide the eye with direction and repetition without competing with the central message.

Review at the target size and in context. Check copy length, image crops, backgrounds, screen sizes, and accessibility. Give evidence-based findings: identify an element, explain its effect on comprehension or attention, and recommend an adjustment. Distinguish usability problems from taste preferences. Do not force every principle onto every design.

Use `graphic-design` for visual assets, `ux-ui-design` for interactions, `web-design` for websites, and `motion-design` for animation.
