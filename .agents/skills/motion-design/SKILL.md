# Motion Design

Use motion to communicate change, guide attention, establish continuity, or express tone. Begin with the message, audience, medium, and user control. Choose styles because they support the content, not because an effect is available.

## Style vocabulary

Treat these as starting points, not presets. Tune to the project and combine sparingly:

1. Glitch cut — brief digital distortion or RGB split for deliberate disruption.
2. Film grain retro — grain, scratches, and warm grading for an aged-film feel.
3. Neon line art — glowing outlines against darker fields.
4. 3D pop-out — layered parallax that brings foreground objects toward the viewer.
5. VHS warp — horizontal jitter, color bleed, optional timecode motifs.
6. Painterly brush — strokes reveal or transform an image.
7. Minimal smooth — restrained movement and polished easing.
8. Kinetic typography — animated type timed to speech, music, or emphasis.
9. Comic halftone — dotted shading, bold outlines, panel-like cuts.
10. Liquid morph — organic shape transitions.
11. Cel-shaded cartoon — flat color, strong outlines, stepped timing.
12. Futuristic HUD — translucent interface overlays and scan-line motifs.
13. Strobe jump — rapid cuts or flashes for impact; use caution and provide reduced-motion alternatives.
14. Stop-motion faux — stepped frames and tactile texture.
15. Color-shift duotone — a limited two-color look with intentional palette changes.
16. Sketch overlay — animated drawing over imagery.
17. Lens-flare bloom — cinematic highlight effects.
18. Mosaic pixelate — block transitions resolving into detail.
19. Mirror symmetry — mirrored or kaleidoscopic composition.
20. Vibe blur — soft blur, glow, or retiming for a dreamy mood.

## Design workflow

1. Define motion's job: orientation, state change, hierarchy, feedback, story, or atmosphere.
2. Choose one primary style and a restrained set of supporting effects. Describe what moves, what stays still, and the trigger.
3. Specify duration, delay, easing, direction, and overlap as prototype values; tune against real content and devices.
4. Preserve readability and user control. Avoid moving text while people need to read it.
5. Check reduced motion, pause/stop controls where needed, flashing risk, touch/keyboard operation, and performance.
6. Compare the motion-off experience; it should still convey essential content and state.

For scroll-linked motion, explain the relation between scroll and progress, preserve an exit, and keep content reachable. Avoid forced movement, surprise zooms, or effects that compete with the task.

For a concept, give a motion sequence, style rationale, timing ranges to test, and accessibility/performance notes. In critique, cite visible behavior, its effect, and a practical adjustment.
