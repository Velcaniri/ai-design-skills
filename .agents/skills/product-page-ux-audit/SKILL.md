# Product Page UX Audit

## Purpose

Use this skill to critically review e-commerce product pages and
recommend practical improvements. Evaluate the page as a reusable
shopping experience---not just as a single attractive screenshot.

The goal is to make the product easier to understand, evaluate, trust,
and purchase while keeping the interface clear, consistent, accessible,
and visually coherent.

## When to use

Activate this skill when the user asks to:

-   Critique or audit an e-commerce product page.
-   Find UX/UI mistakes in a product detail page.
-   Redesign a product page or improve its visual hierarchy.
-   Review a product-page screenshot, prototype, or implementation.
-   Create a prioritized list of fixes and enhancements for a shopping
    flow.

## Core principles

1.  **Design for a system, not one screenshot.** The page must work with
    different products, images, names, prices, discounts, quantities,
    and content lengths.
2.  **Prioritize clarity over decoration.** Every visual choice should
    help users understand the product or complete a task.
3.  **Build a clear hierarchy.** Product identity, rating, price,
    options, and the primary purchase action should be easy to find.
4.  **Keep related actions together.** For example, quantity selection
    should be close to the add-to-cart action.
5.  **Reduce uncertainty.** Make the selected quantity, unit, and price
    clear before the user commits.
6.  **Use consistency deliberately.** Alignment, spacing, typography,
    colors, icons, and imagery should follow a coherent system.
7.  **Preserve flexibility.** Recommendations should account for the
    product category and buying context; do not apply every pattern
    mechanically.
8.  **Explain the UX reason.** Do not merely say that something looks
    bad. Explain how it affects comprehension, scanning, confidence,
    accessibility, or task completion.

## Audit checklist: 15 common mistakes

Use this checklist as a starting point. Report only issues that are
actually present. Do not force all 15 findings onto every page.

### 1. Navigation icons lack contrast over the product image

**Look for:** Navigation or utility icons placed directly over product
imagery without a stable contrast treatment.

**Why it matters:** Their visibility may depend on the brightness,
color, or complexity of the current image. A design that works with one
image may fail with another.

**Improve it by:** - Placing icons on a subtle, consistent background
container. - Ensuring sufficient contrast in every relevant state. -
Using a restrained outline or other separation treatment when needed. -
Testing against bright, dark, busy, and simple product images.

### 2. Product imagery is poorly chosen or inconsistent

**Look for:** Images that are too busy, lack a clear focal point,
emphasize the wrong subject, misrepresent how the product is sold, feel
artificial, or clash with the rest of the catalog.

**Why it matters:** Product imagery strongly influences first
impressions and trust. Images also need to work together in product
grids and recommendation lists.

**Improve it by:** - Making the product itself the clear focal point. -
Choosing an image that accurately represents the product and its selling
format. - Using natural, believable styling appropriate to the
product. - Establishing consistent lighting, backgrounds, framing, and
visual treatment across the catalog. - Considering both the individual
product page and the wider shopping experience.

### 3. Inconsistent alignment

**Look for:** Text, icons, prices, buttons, and sections that do not
follow shared margins or a clear layout grid.

**Why it matters:** Misalignment makes the page feel less structured and
harder to scan, even when users cannot identify the exact cause.

**Improve it by:** - Defining a consistent grid and page margins. -
Aligning related elements to shared edges. - Applying spacing rules
consistently across sections and screen sizes.

### 4. Overly bright or saturated colors

**Look for:** Too many strong colors or emphasis colors competing for
attention.

**Why it matters:** Excessive saturation can reduce readability and
weaken hierarchy because too many elements demand attention.

**Improve it by:** - Using a restrained palette suited to the product
and brand. - Reserving stronger colors for meaningful actions or
statuses. - Ensuring text and controls maintain adequate contrast. -
Letting product imagery remain visually prominent.

### 5. Inconsistent typography

**Look for:** Too many font families or arbitrary combinations of size,
weight, color, and line height.

**Why it matters:** Inconsistent type makes the page feel less cohesive
and makes information hierarchy harder to understand.

**Improve it by:** - Using one well-chosen font family in most cases. -
Creating hierarchy with size, weight, color, and line height. - Defining
reusable text styles for titles, labels, body copy, metadata, and
buttons.

### 6. Cramped labels and overloaded badges

**Look for:** Small uppercase labels with insufficient letter spacing,
or badges containing redundant words, icons, and decorative details.

**Why it matters:** Labels should be understood quickly. Overloaded
badges become noisy and compete with important product information.

**Improve it by:** - Adding modest letter spacing to small uppercase
labels where appropriate. - Keeping badge copy short and specific. -
Removing redundant wording and unnecessary icons. - Making sure discount
information is immediately understandable.

### 7. Weak product-title hierarchy

**Look for:** A title that is too large, too heavy, too small, visually
unbalanced, or poorly integrated with nearby information.

**Why it matters:** The title identifies what the user is considering
buying and should be easy to find without overwhelming the page.

**Improve it by:** - Giving the title a clear but controlled visual
hierarchy. - Adjusting size and weight to suit the layout. - Keeping the
title distinct from labels and supporting details.

### 8. Difficult-to-read description text

**Look for:** Body copy with tight line spacing, excessive contrast,
long uninterrupted blocks, or poor text measure.

**Why it matters:** Descriptions should support understanding, not
compete with the title or feel tiring to read.

**Improve it by:** - Increasing line height when lines feel crowded. -
Using comfortable body-text contrast. - Breaking content into scannable
paragraphs or structured details where useful. - Keeping headline
styling distinct from supporting copy.

### 9. Rating and review information is detached from the title

**Look for:** Ratings or review counts placed far from the product name.

**Why it matters:** Users often look for the product identity and its
social proof together when evaluating whether an item is trustworthy.

**Improve it by:** - Placing rating and review information near the
title when the layout allows. - Making the rating value and review count
clear. - Linking the rating to the review section if the interface
supports that interaction.

### 10. Inconsistent feature icons

**Look for:** Feature or benefit icons with unrelated colors, stroke
weights, filled/outlined styles, sizes, or visual complexity.

**Why it matters:** Inconsistent iconography creates visual noise and
can make a supporting feature section dominate the page.

**Improve it by:** - Choosing a consistent icon family or visual
language. - Standardizing size, stroke weight, and treatment. - Using
color intentionally rather than assigning arbitrary colors. - Keeping
feature explanations concise and easy to scan.

### 11. Heavy divider lines

**Look for:** Dark or thick separators that attract more attention than
the content they separate.

**Why it matters:** Overly strong dividers make the interface feel
fragmented into harsh blocks.

**Improve it by:** - Using subtle separators. - Relying on spacing and
grouping where possible. - Reserving stronger boundaries for cases where
they genuinely improve comprehension.

### 12. Unbalanced section spacing

**Look for:** Large gaps that disconnect related sections, or cramped
spacing that makes unrelated content run together.

**Why it matters:** Spacing communicates relationships. Too much or too
little space can disrupt the reading flow.

**Improve it by:** - Defining a consistent spacing scale. - Keeping
related content visually grouped. - Using larger gaps to distinguish
major sections. - Checking the full page flow rather than adjusting each
gap in isolation.

### 13. Redundant price labels

**Look for:** Labels such as "Price" immediately before an obvious
currency amount, when the context already makes the meaning clear.

**Why it matters:** Redundant labels consume space and can create
inconsistent labeling if nearby controls are not labeled in the same
way.

**Improve it by:** - Removing labels that add no useful information. -
Keeping explicit labels when they resolve genuine ambiguity or support
accessibility. - Applying labeling logic consistently across the
interface.

### 14. Confusing product quantity and price presentation

**Look for:** - A fixed quantity embedded in the product title even
though users can change the selected quantity. - Price information
placed too far from the product identity or purchase controls. - Unclear
units, pricing basis, or relationship between quantity and total cost.

**Why it matters:** Users need to understand what the product is, what
quantity they are selecting, and how much that selection costs.

**Improve it by:** - Keeping the title focused on the product
identity. - Handling selectable quantity in the purchase area. - Showing
price early enough to support evaluation. - Making units and pricing
basis explicit when needed. - Updating the displayed total when quantity
changes.

### 15. Poorly designed purchase action

**Look for:** - An add-to-cart button that is visually aggressive or
competes with everything else. - Quantity controls placed far away from
the purchase button. - Missing or unclear unit information. - No clear
indication of the total cost before the action.

**Why it matters:** Choosing a quantity and adding it to the cart are
parts of the same task. Users should understand what they are adding and
what it will cost.

**Improve it by:** - Making the primary action prominent without making
it shout. - Placing quantity selection beside or close to the
add-to-cart action. - Showing the unit directly in or beside the
quantity control. - Displaying the calculated total where appropriate. -
Ensuring the button label clearly communicates the action.

## Further UX enhancements

After fixing actual usability and consistency problems, consider whether
these enhancements fit the product and platform.

### A. Scrollable product-details card

A product-details panel can sit in a rounded, subtly elevated card that
moves over part of the product image as the user scrolls.

Consider: - Giving the image and details distinct visual areas. -
Keeping enough of the image visible early in the interaction. -
Transitioning the product title into a sticky header only if it improves
orientation. - Avoiding unnecessary motion or layouts that obscure
content.

### B. Sticky bottom purchase area

Keep quantity selection and the add-to-cart action accessible while
users scroll through longer product information.

Consider: - Reserving enough screen space so the sticky area does not
cover content. - Respecting mobile safe areas and keyboard behavior. -
Ensuring the displayed price and quantity stay synchronized with the
page state. - Testing whether the sticky controls help on the target
device rather than assuming they always do.

### C. Predefined quantity options

Offer common quantities as one-tap choices, such as 500 g, 1 kg, or 2
kg, when those options make sense for the product.

Consider: - Using actual customer purchase patterns or business rules to
choose presets. - Keeping a custom quantity control if users need
flexibility. - Clearly showing the selected option and resulting
total. - Supporting products sold by item, weight, volume, or other
units without forcing one quantity model onto all products.

## Recommended audit workflow

### Step 1: Understand the context

Identify, if available: - Product category and how it is sold. - Target
users and their likely shopping goals. - Platform and viewport (mobile,
tablet, desktop). - Brand and design-system constraints. - Relevant
interactions, such as image galleries, variants, quantity selection, and
cart behavior.

If context is missing, state reasonable assumptions rather than
inventing facts.

### Step 2: Inspect the complete experience

Review more than the initial screenshot when possible: - Initial
viewport and visual hierarchy. - Product images and image-gallery
controls. - Title, rating, reviews, description, and feature
information. - Variants, quantity controls, price, and purchase
action. - Scrolling behavior and sticky elements. - Responsive layouts
and important interaction states.

If only a screenshot is provided, distinguish visible findings from
things that require interaction or additional screens to verify.

### Step 3: Identify and explain issues

For every finding: 1. Name the issue. 2. Point to the affected element
or location. 3. Explain why it is a problem for the user. 4. Recommend a
concrete correction. 5. Assign a priority.

Do not invent defects to reach a target number. The 15-item checklist is
a diagnostic reference, not a quota.

### Step 4: Prioritize

Use this priority guide:

-   **Critical:** Prevents or seriously disrupts understanding or
    purchase completion; may involve a broken core interaction or
    misleading price/quantity information.
-   **High:** Creates substantial confusion, weakens trust, or makes a
    key action difficult to find or use.
-   **Medium:** Harms readability, scanning, consistency, or responsive
    behavior but has a workable path around it.
-   **Low:** A minor visual refinement with limited effect on task
    completion.

Explain the reasoning behind priority rather than assigning labels
arbitrarily.

### Step 5: Separate fixes from enhancements

First address problems that impair clarity, trust, accessibility, or
task completion. Then propose optional enhancements such as sticky
purchase controls, animated transitions, or quantity presets.

Do not introduce complexity merely to make the redesign look more
sophisticated.

### Step 6: Validate the recommendations

Where implementation or testing is possible, check: - Contrast and
legibility across different product images. - Alignment and spacing
across page sections. - Long and short product titles and
descriptions. - Different prices, discounts, and currencies. - Different
quantity units and selected quantities. - Responsive behavior and
sticky-control overlap. - Keyboard access, focus visibility, and
screen-reader labeling. - Whether the total price is correct and updates
when inputs change.

## Response format

Unless the user requests another format, structure an audit like this:

# Product Page UX Audit

**Overall assessment:** A concise, candid summary of the page's main
strengths and weaknesses.

## Findings

For each issue:

**\[Priority\] Finding title**

-   **Where:** The affected element or area.
-   **Problem:** What is wrong.
-   **User impact:** Why it matters.
-   **Recommendation:** What to change.

## Recommended fix order

List the highest-impact fixes first, with a short reason for the order.

## Optional enhancements

Separate ideas that improve convenience or polish from fixes required to
resolve existing problems.

## What needs verification

List anything that cannot be confirmed from the supplied screenshot or
materials.

## Tone and quality bar

-   Be direct, specific, and constructive.
-   Be honest when a design choice is poor, but explain the reason.
-   Avoid vague comments such as "make it cleaner" or "improve the UX."
-   Avoid treating personal taste as an objective usability defect.
-   Do not assume every product page should use the same layout.
-   Do not recommend a pattern without explaining the user benefit and
    relevant trade-off.
-   Keep recommendations implementable and proportionate to the problem.
-   When useful, distinguish visual polish from functional UX problems.

## Source-derived foundation

This skill is based on a product-page redesign walkthrough that
